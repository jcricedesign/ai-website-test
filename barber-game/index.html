01 / Ambiguity

Start before the product is clear.

The Barber Game began with a broad idea around helping people find barbers. The useful work was figuring out what it should actually become.

Rather than jump directly into features or screens, I worked across the team to expose assumptions, understand the business, talk with barbers and clients, and make the competing possibilities visible.

02 / Alignment

Get everything on the table.

I started by getting the team into the same room and giving everyone an equal opportunity to define what BarberGame could be.

We captured different perspectives, problems, opportunities and ideas together. Then we organized them, looked for patterns and made the tradeoffs visible.

The product began to emerge from the conversation.

03 / Research

Listen for the human problem.

We interviewed barbers, clients and people around the business. What emerged wasn't a request for more technology. It was a set of very human problems: gaps, no-shows, unclear expectations, trust, time and finding the right person.

“Not once has a barber said, ‘I wish I had AI.’”

The research redirected the product away from a generic feature race and toward the relationship between barber and client.

04 / Product definition

A companion, not an operating system.

Everyone else was trying to become everything.

Scheduling. CRM. Marketing. Payments. Payroll. Inventory. The category was converging on software that wanted to run the entire business.

We found a more useful direction: help the barber's business succeed wherever it already runs. Integrate where appropriate. Preserve choice. Keep proving value instead of demanding exclusivity.

05 / From why → how

Carry the idea all the way down.

The strategy became product behavior: profiles, availability, appointment states, open slots, communication, reputation, recognition and ways to understand the future of the barber's business.

I worked from those models into flows, interface systems, prototypes and front-end experiences—moving repeatedly between the 10,000-foot product question and the ten-foot implementation detail.

06 / Principal role

Make the work inclusive, visible and buildable.

The job wasn't to have all the answers. It was to create a process where the right answers could emerge.

That meant bringing design, business and engineering into the work early; making research and tradeoffs transparent; giving people a meaningful way to contribute; and then turning what we learned into a coherent direction the team could actually build.
